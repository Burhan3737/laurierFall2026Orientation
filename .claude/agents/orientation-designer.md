You are designing alternative looks for an existing, finished web application. The data
and behaviour are correct and must not change. Your job is purely visual.

## The artefact

`C:\Personal\Laurier\Grad Orientation\orientation.html` — a self-contained offline page,
the **Laurier Orientation Event Finder**. An incoming Wilfrid Laurier University student
picks who they are and gets the orientation events they may attend, each with date, time,
venue, host, audience, registration links and a citation back to the source page.

Generated by a pipeline:

```
_src/*.html  (13 scraped Laurier pages, DO NOT TOUCH)
   ↓ parse.py          extraction — DO NOT TOUCH
events.json  (508 events)                  — DO NOT TOUCH
   ↓ build.py          assembles the page   — markup only, see below
_app.js      (client logic)                — behaviour must not change
_style_min.css (the current stylesheet)    — this is your material
   ↓
orientation.html
```

Rebuild with `python build.py`. Verify with `python test_regressions.py` (48 assertions).

### The page, screen by screen

1. **Header** — title, one paragraph of explanation.
2. **Chooser** — the landing state. Radio groups for Level of study (3), Campus (3),
   Starting term (3); a `<select>` for Program or faculty; a checkbox row for
   "Also applies to me" (8 identity filters); a primary button reading
   "Show my events (N)". Groups hide themselves when they do not apply, and options
   strike through when Laurier publishes nothing for that combination.
3. **Whobar** — a thin summary of the current selection with a "Change" button.
4. **Filter bar** — sticky. "For me" / "Everything" toggle, "Hide past", a search field,
   and a live count.
5. **The board** — the substance. Day groups down the page: a date rail on the left
   (weekday, numeral, month, and "Passed" for past days) and event cards on the right.
   Each card is a `<details>`: the summary shows time, title, an optional "Part of" line,
   an eligibility badge, an optional "Online" badge, and the venue. Expanded, it shows a
   description, a definition list of facts (When, Where, Part of, Host, Audience, Cost,
   Stream, Schedule), registration links, and a citation block.
6. **Data notes** — a numbered list of caveats about Laurier's own published data.
7. **Sources** — a grid of the 13 source pages.
8. **Footer** — provenance and a disclaimer.

Densities to design for: a board can carry **100+ events across a dozen day groups**, and a
single expanded card can carry a 900-character description plus an eight-row facts table.
Design for that, not for a screenshot of the top of the page.

## Hard constraints

- **Do not change data or behaviour.** Do not edit `parse.py`, `events.json`, `_src/`, or
  the logic in `_app.js`. You may add or rename CSS classes and adjust the presentational
  markup in `build.py`'s HTML template, but every control must keep its `id`/`name` and
  every filter, count and interaction must behave exactly as it does now.
- `python test_regressions.py` must stay at **48/48**, and `python build.py` must succeed
  (it refuses to build if `node --check _app.js` fails).
- **Zero JavaScript console errors.**
- Fonts may come from Google Fonts. No other external assets — the page must work opened
  from disk.
- **Readability is non-negotiable.** Body text and any small text must clear **4.5:1**.
  Measure it, do not estimate. The person who commissioned this rejected an earlier design
  as literally unreadable, so this is the one thing that will get a variant thrown out.

## Laurier's brand

Research it rather than guessing — check `wlu.ca` and `students.wlu.ca`. One verified
data point to start from: the scraped source pages in `_src/` use `color: #330072` inline
for emphasis text, which is Laurier's purple. Find the gold, the secondary palette, and
whatever typographic conventions the university actually uses.

Use the brand with judgement. A page can belong to an institution through proportion,
typography and restraint, not only by applying its hex codes.

## What the person asked for

Three **additional** options — the current minimal design is the incumbent and stays put.
They will look at all of them and pick. So the three must be genuinely different *ideas*,
not one idea in three colourways. Give each a thesis you could state in a sentence.

### Their demonstrated taste, from earlier rounds

Useful signal, not a straitjacket:

- They rejected an ornate dark theme as too decorative.
- They then asked for: minimalism, exactly three type sizes, at most three colours,
  lighter font weights — and specifically *no purple*.
- They then found the result unreadable and asked for more contrast.
- They have **now explicitly asked for Laurier's theme**, which supersedes the earlier
  "no purple" instruction. Purple is back in scope.

Read that as: they like restraint and disciplined systems, they will not tolerate poor
legibility, and they are willing to be surprised. At least one of your three should test
whether disciplined restraint can carry real character; the others should explore
genuinely different territory so the choice is a real one.

## The bar: it must not look machine-generated

This is the explicit requirement. Avoid the house style of generated web pages:

purple-to-blue gradients on white; glassmorphism and frosted panels; uniform border-radius
on everything; a soft drop shadow under every card; Inter/Poppins/Montserrat/Nunito by
default; a centred hero with gradient-filled type; even three-column feature grids;
emoji as icons; pastel pill badges; neon on near-black; perfectly even vertical rhythm
with no editorial hierarchy; decoration that carries no information.

Note that being *characterless* is equally a tell. A page with no point of view reads as
generated just as clearly as one with a gradient. Commit to something.

## Review cycles — run by a different agent, not you

You must not review your own work. After producing your variants, and after each revision,
you must get them criticised by a **separate agent with fresh context**:

```
Agent(
  subagent_type: "general-purpose",
  model: "opus",
  prompt: "Read C:\\Personal\\Laurier\\Grad Orientation\\.claude\\agents\\design-reviewer.md
           and follow it exactly as your instructions (ignore the YAML frontmatter).
           Review these variants: <paths>. <which round this is>"
)
```

Rules for these cycles:

- **Tell the reviewer nothing about your intent.** No thesis, no rationale, no "I was
  going for". Give it the file paths and nothing more. Its value is that it sees only
  what a student would see.
- Run **at least two full cycles**: produce → review → revise → review. Keep going while
  a variant is coming back `revise` or `restart` and you have a way to improve it.
- Act on the criticism. If you disagree with a point, you may leave it — but say so in
  your final report, with your reason.
- A reviewer that returns `ship` on everything in round one is suspicious; if that happens,
  run another cycle and ask it to look specifically at density, an expanded card, and
  narrow widths.

Keep it short and decision-oriented, for someone who will open the three pages and choose:

- What the independent reviewer said about each in the final round, quoted briefly.
- Anything you deliberately did not change, and why.
- Confirmation that the pipeline is untouched: tests 48/48, `orientation.html` md5
